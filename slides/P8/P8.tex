   \frametitle{What is a model?}
   \only<1>{
      \begin{figure}
         \includegraphics[width = .3\textwidth]{vogue.jpg}
         \caption{from \href{https://archive.vogue.com/issue/18921224}{\textcolor{blue}{Vogue}} (December 24, 1892)}

   \only<2>{
      \begin{block}{Model}
         In simple terms a model is a \underline{simplified} representation of a system (reality) that helps to understand how the system works/worked in the past/will work in the future.
      \end{block}
   }
   \only<3,4>{
      \begin{itemize}
         \item What is a model? \href{https://youtu.be/RK9m4OmFAbY?feature=shared}{\textcolor{blue}{YouTube video}}
         \item<4> Formalizing a theory into a model allows the researcher to
         describe their ideas in a precise, unambiguous way (Goldstone \& Janssen 2005; Epstein 2008). 
         \item<4> Models are conceptually precise, their assumptions are clear;
         they allow formal deduction and an easy way to verify their internal
         validity (Timpone \& Taber 1996). 
         \item<4> Last but not least they provide an unambiguous way to
         communicate within the scientific community (Nowak, Rychwalska, \&
         Borkowski, 2015).
      \end{itemize}

   \only<6>{
      \framesubtitle{What are computer simulations?}
      \begin{block}{Computer Simulation}
         Computer simulation is the running of a model on a computer, the model
         being designed to represent the behaviour of, or the outcome of, a
         real-world or physical system. The reliability of some models
         can be determined by comparing their results to the real-world outcomes
         they aim to predict/explain.
      \end{block}
   }

   \end{enumerate}

\begin{frame}
   \frametitle{Types of Computer Simulations}

   \begin{itemize}
      \item Key characteristics of models we want to investigate include:
      \begin{enumerate}
         \item intrinsic dynamics: the natural evolution of the system over time without external influences.
         \item nonlinearity: the system's output is not directly proportional to its input, leading to complex behavior.
      \end{enumerate}
      \item Computer simulations allow us to:
      \begin{enumerate}
         \item Capture evolution of the system over time;
         \item Capture the system's nonlinear complexity.
      \end{enumerate}
      \item We will focus on two main types of computer simulations: agent-based models and cellular automata.
   \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
   \frametitle{Agent-based models}
   \begin{block}{Agent-based models}
      Agent-based models are discrete-time computer simulations used to study
      the interactions between people, things, places, and time. They are
      stochastic models built from the bottom up meaning individual agents
      (often people in epidemiology) are assigned certain attributes.
      \alert{The agents are programmed to behave and interact with other
      agents and the environment in certain ways.} These interactions produce
      emergent effects that may differ from effects of individual agents.
   \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Bar Dynamics}
\begin{frame}
   \frametitle{When is it worth going to a bar?}
   \only<+>{
      \begin{figure}
         \includegraphics[width = .65\textwidth]{barstudio.jpeg}
      \end{figure}
   }
   \only<+>{
      \begin{columns}
         \begin{column}{.49\textwidth}
            A simple model of bar attendance:
            \begin{itemize}
               \item The bar has a fixed capacity of people;
               \item If the bar is full, nobody likes;
               \item The decision to go to the bar depends on how crowded it was the previous day.
               
            \end{itemize}
         \end{column}
         \begin{column}{.49\textwidth}
            \begin{figure}
               \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{barstudio.jpeg}
               \caption{Bar Studio}
            \end{figure}
         \end{column}
      \end{columns}
   }
   \only<+>{
      \begin{columns}
         \begin{column}{.59\textwidth}
            Initial occupancy:
            $$ ba_{0} = 0.2 $$
            Word of mouth growth (order parameter):
            $$ ba_{t+1} = r \times ba_{t} $$
            Constraint for overcrowding (control parameter):
            $$ ba_{t+1} = (1 - ba_{t}) $$
            Bar Dynamics:
            $$ ba_{t+1} = r \times ba_{t} \times (1 - ba_{t}) $$
            
         \end{column}
         \begin{column}{.39\textwidth}
            \begin{figure}
               \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{barstudio.jpeg}
               \caption{Bar Studio}
            \end{figure}
         \end{column}
      \end{columns}
   }
   \only<+>{
      \begin{tikzpicture}
         \node at (0,0) {\includegraphics[width = .4\textwidth]{bar_dynamics_stable.png}};
         \node at (7,0) {\includegraphics[width = .4\textwidth]{bar_dynamics_cyclic.png}};
         \node at (3.5,-3.5) {\includegraphics[width = .4\textwidth]{bar_dynamics_chaotic.png}};
      \end{tikzpicture}
   }
   \only<+>{
      Dynamical systems may be described by a system of equations that accounts for change over time. These equations include two types of parameters:

      \begin{columns}
         \begin{column}{.49\textwidth}
            \begin{block}{Order parameters}
               Tells you what the system is doing over time (its dynamics).

            \end{block}
         \end{column}
         \begin{column}{.49\textwidth}
            \begin{block}{Control parameters}
               Tells you under what conditions the system does so (its constraints).
            \end{block}
         \end{column}
      \end{columns}
   }
   \only<+>{
      \begin{quote}
         In nonlinear dynamics, smooth and \alert{small changes in control parameters may lead to dramatic and unpredictable changes in behavior.} (...) Radical change in the pattern usually follows from a change in control parameters and thus is an indicator of external influences.
         \medskip
         \par\nobreak\hfill — \textit{Nowak \& Lewenstein, 1994}
      \end{quote}
   }

\end{frame}
